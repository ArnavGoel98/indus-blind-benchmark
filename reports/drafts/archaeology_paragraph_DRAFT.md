**Archaeological variables behind the map (draft).** The two axes of the decipherability map are
not abstract. Both are set by how an Indus corpus is assembled. Duplication depends on object type:
at Harappa, seals are "almost all … unique", while incised tablets carry copies of the same text and
molded tablets come in same-mold duplicates. One context held script copies on 22 incised steatite
tablets and another held 31 molded terracotta duplicates (Kenoyer & Meadow 2010, pp. 6-7). All of
these objects come from secondary contexts: trash, fill and street deposits (Kenoyer & Meadow 2010,
p. 7; Kenoyer 2020b, p. 251). Inventory size depends on time depth: the conventional 400-450 signs
pool about 700 years of use, and each sub-period may have used "considerably less" (Kenoyer 2020b,
p. 249). On our synthetic Indus-like corpora, these choices matter as much as any method does.
With a related language among the candidate languages, a seals-like corpus (duplicates near 0)
falls where the best tested method recovers 18-39% of sign tokens, against 4-17% for a
tablets-like corpus (duplicates at or above the pooled M77 rate of 0.354). The contrast holds when
the mix of languages and scripts is held fixed (21-42% against 3-16%). Without a related language,
every region stays at or below about 7%. A single-period corpus would fall below the smallest
inventory we could build, so the map cannot place it, and every such subset also contains fewer
texts than the 2,906 the map assumes. Two limitations bound this reading. The subset positions rest
on qualitative statements and raw counts, not published subset rates. The inventory axis is varied
only through allographs, which no tested method merges. Our conclusion is narrower than a
difficulty estimate: any statement about how hard the Indus script is to decipher must first say
which objects and which period the corpus contains. Building corpora by object type and period, as
Kenoyer & Meadow (2010) advocate, is a precondition for an interpretable benchmark.
